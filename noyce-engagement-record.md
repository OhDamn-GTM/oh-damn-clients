# Part 3\. Voice of customer: the Darren McCarty call (3 August 2026\)

A live demo and sales conversation between Thejas and a real prospect in the exact target ICP. The most useful research in the project, because Darren articulates the value proposition better than either company has.

## Who Darren is

Partner, not founder. His partner Trong Tai founded the agency. Both spent 15 to 20 years at Kelly Services, both went through multiple rounds of layoffs, both were ultimately laid off, and neither wanted to return to corporate, so they started their own agency.

They hold a five-year retained contract with the University of California system covering executive and retained search across nine campuses, the medical facilities and three research labs, from CTO and CIO down to department heads. His caveat: "that sounds great in practice, but we still have to sell into the client base. We still have to get them to trust us and we're a small startup agency. And so we're going against the Korn Ferrys of the world for that retained business."

To keep cash coming in they also run legal recruitment, "a really, really hot market." They source some job orders through Paraform. Trong is separately building an AI interviewer, which Darren tests. On their own identity: "we started this company and now we're like, well, maybe we're a recruiting company, but maybe we're a technology company too."

One licence in use currently. "We plan to scale to 10 people once we get really fully up and running."

## The Juicebox problem in his words

On his top-priority search, a personal injury attorney in Seattle, three to eight years, to lead the office:

> "When I use Juicebox I can't figure out. I'll get all different, like I'll either get thousands of attorneys, all different types of experience and I'm totally off base, or I'll get like two attorneys that actually have personal injury experience."  
>   
> "So Juicebox isn't great for legal recruitment. And that's what I'm running into."

On why precision matters more in legal than in sales roles, which is the clearest statement of the core value proposition anyone has produced:

> "A sales rep will respond to just a blast that says, hey, I've got a position, you want to hear about it? Because they're a sales rep, and they always want to hear about new jobs. An attorney, uh-uh. You have to be spot on with, this is what I've got, this is why I think it's the right spot for you in your career. Because if you're off, they're not going to respond to you."

Thejas on the underlying mechanism: "I know how Juicebox works. It works based on tags. It doesn't work based on a real analysis of the experience. So whenever the tags don't work, it doesn't work. Tags only work in the tech areas, like some programming language."

## The live head-to-head

Search run during the call: personal injury attorney, Seattle and nearby cities, in office five days a week, three plus years capped around fifteen, $180K to $250K. Location, position, sector, skills and experience all set as must-haves.

Noyce surfaced 580 candidates during the call, with results appearing while the search continued. The exported set was 315 above the match threshold.

Darren ran the same search in Juicebox in parallel and checked each Noyce result against it:

- **Ivan Cameron**, 100% match, trial and complex litigation attorney. "That guy's like a 100% match. So I don't even need to. I'm looking through the 100% matches and \[Juicebox\] did not source him." Confirmed a good match.  
- **Jessica**, 100% match, associate at Grube and Barr. Juicebox did not source her.  
- **Colin Hutchinson Fleming**, 100% match, Davis Law Group. "Yeah, see it didn't source them either."  
- **James Chong**, 100% match, looked wrong on the surface. The fit score reasoning said his current firm, Hagens Berman, is a plaintiff-side personal injury mass litigation firm. They checked the firm's website live and the reasoning held. "There it is. Yeah. So it got it right."

Darren identified the mechanism himself, unprompted:

> "It actually was smart enough to take the law firms that she worked for and realise that those are \[PI firms\]. And that's the thing that Juicebox doesn't do. It doesn't take it to the next level."

And described his manual workaround, which is the product spec:

> "What I would have to do in order to get this candidate is run a search for all the different law firms in the area that do PI work and for associate and then add all those law firms in. But then the problem is that the data they purchase doesn't necessarily have those candidates in it."

Then, separately and without prompting:

> "There's times where I'm like, I wish I could just scrape the web for every PI firm in Seattle and then just have it look through the lawyers that work there, just to find that information. But I don't have the time to click on every single one. It's just hours."

## The price conversation

Thejas quoted per-seat pricing, four tiers:

- Teams, three to five seats: flat **$999 per month** for 1,500 queries and 1,500 contacts per month.  
- Beyond five seats: **$1,667 per month**, which is $20K per year, effectively unlimited queries.  
- Individual seats: deliberately on hold. "For an individual seat we are only focusing on teams and enterprises right now."

Thejas positioned that against Juicebox's entry tier: "for $999 per month it's 1,500 queries, much higher than what Juicebox offers. So I'm directly going against, the lowest tier is $99 per month."

Darren's counter: "Juicebox actually gives us unlimited. We got in early and it's like $200 a month."

Then the line that is the whole argument for this ICP:

> "Now to me, there's value in $200 a month, but if I'm not getting good search results, it's $200 a month, but if I'm not getting good search results, what does it matter? It could be free and it doesn't matter because then it's just wasting my time."

He asked for $400 a month with unlimited sourcing credits. Thejas declined unlimited, since credits cost money, and counter-offered $400 per month for 500 credits plus a nominal per-credit fee beyond. Darren: "I could possibly get behind that."

On the switching barrier: "I'm getting okayish. I'm getting enough to where we won't cancel Juicebox because we don't have an alternative." He asked for a month's trial; Thejas offered a week.

## Feature gap found live

Darren pre-writes five or six emails himself and sets a custom cadence by hand: first email, then two business days, then three, then four, then five. Only the second is a nudge; the third changes tack to "I know this position might not be the right fit for you, but let's at least talk about what I'm seeing in the market."

Why he writes them himself: "you can tell when something's written by AI, and lawyers..."

Noyce's nudge is automatic and not customisable. Juicebox has the multi-step custom sequence. Thejas: "currently the nudge is not customisable, but I can ship that to you in like no time." Darren named this as a condition alongside the trial.

## Other facts from the call

- Company name origin: named after Robert Noyce, "the founder of Silicon Valley."  
- Thejas's stated focus: "a recruitment platform for agency recruiters, very specifically in non-tech areas. Manufacturing, healthcare, legal, all of those areas where there is currently what I see as very underserved areas."  
- Product scope as pitched: "it is all in one thing. So it is your top of the funnel, it's your outreach platform and it's your ATS. Again, we are not insisting that you use it for all three."  
- A marketing mechanic already running: Thejas invites recruiters to challenge the platform. "If my platform beats Juicebox, I actually send them something, or rather if Juicebox beats my platform, I send them something." He also gives away the exported result set for any search a recruiter brings.  
- The Seattle personal injury search is one of Noyce's marquee demos, described as a unicorn search. Darren arrived with essentially the same live requirement by coincidence.  
- Cancel anytime, no strings.

## Competitive intelligence: why Juicebox actually retains customers

The clip titled "Why Juicebox Sells" is more valuable than its name suggests, because it explains the real switching barrier, and it is not search quality.

The recruiter pays $100 a month for their own account plus two colleagues, $300 a month total. Asked whether they would still use Juicebox if it withheld candidate emails and capped outreach at 30 messages like LinkedIn:

> "If they are not giving me email and they are also restricted with the 30 emails like LinkedIn, then I have to think, why I have to be the customer for them, because then LinkedIn gives me more visibility compared to Juicebox. So Juicebox I am using because I am getting \[the emails\]."

Juicebox is retained for unrestricted contact data and outreach, not match quality, which the same recruiters trash elsewhere. This lines up exactly with Darren McCarty naming the custom email sequence builder as a condition of purchase. Contact data and outreach freedom are table stakes, not differentiators.

## The enablement conversation

Mike Sienkowski on the layer above sourcing. He frames the limiting factors as time and "who's likely to get back to you," then lists the response-likelihood signals he uses from experience: has the person switched jobs in the last year, have they stayed anywhere more than three or four years, "if they're two years two years two years do you really want that person, it's probably not the company, at some point it's them," have they hit their four-year vesting cliff, and have they done it twice. On someone with ten years across three FAANG companies being recruited for an early startup: "don't bother."

His conclusion is the product spec for that layer:

> "If you can accurately dump as much of that signal into a product, with a nice interface, that candidate pool is likely to get back to you... you could probably winnow it down to a list that is better than they're going to see elsewhere."

# Part 5\. The decks

## Noyce pitch deck

**Brand story.** Named after Robert Noyce, co-founder of Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel, 1927 to 1990\. The framing: "From the Fairchildren, to Xerox PARC, to the Homebrew Club, to the PayPal Mafia, to the founding of OpenAI, the story of Silicon Valley has always been the same story: the right talent, in the right room, can spark a revolution."

Existing taglines: "Assembling Legendary Teams" and "The AI-Native Talent Partner."

**Market framing.** 70M hiring transactions a year in the US. At a conservative $5,000 per hire that is a $350B market. Fifty years without fundamental change: rolodexes, email, ATS, LinkedIn and Indeed, SaaS, AI, wave after wave, and median recruiter output is still one placement per month, with two being an outlier.

**The wedge.** Sourcing, roughly 60% of recruiter time and the bottleneck for every later funnel stage. Be the best sourcing platform in the world first, then expand. Three layers with market sizes: Sourcing Platform ($12B), Recruiter Enablement ($40B), Digital Workers ($150B+). The sourcing layer lists "outcome-aligned per-search" pricing.

**The competitive claim.** "In head-to-head duel performed by expert recruiters, Noyce beat LinkedIn and Juicebox by a mile. It was not even close."

Competitors named: LinkedIn Recruiter, "Boolean search, frozen since 2008," $45B+ parent market cap. Juicebox, "NL search, tag based matching," $115M total raised. Long tail: Findem, SeekOut, hireEZ, Gem, "all chasing the same Boolean \+ keyword paradigm. None do deep research."

Four stated differentiators: agents read the full context of every candidate including papers, projects, commits and talks; **8 out of 10 candidates worth reaching out to**; works across every segment including tech, healthcare, education, manufacturing, construction and veterinary; natural language with nothing to learn.

**The head-to-head study.** Eight recruiters across six-plus verticals, Noyce side by side against LinkedIn Recruiter and Juicebox:

## Industry deck: "The Recruitment Industry: 21 things almost nobody knows" (May 2026\)

A sourced thought-leadership deck, every insight cited. Usable as marketing content in its own right.

Headline framing: recruiting is a $180B industry of 67 million annual transactions with no category leader, no margin to invest in itself, and a workflow unchanged since 1970\.

**Tech is 2.5% of hires but 80% of the attention.** Tech and information is about 2.5% of all US hires, yet captures most of the industry's mindshare, capital and tooling. Leisure and hospitality is 21%, retail 16%, healthcare 14%, construction 8%, manufacturing 7%. This is the strongest available justification for Noyce's non-tech focus.

**The scale nobody sees.** 67M US hires a year, more than mortgages, autos and existing home sales combined (roughly 6M, 16M and 4M).

**No category leader.** Top four recruiting firms hold about 16% share, against 95% for the Big Four in audit, 65% for MBB in strategy, 55% for the bulge bracket in M\&A.

**The ICP, sized.** About 26,000 staffing firms in the US. The top ten hold under 29%. The remaining 71% is a long tail of sub-$10M shops.

**The margin trap.** Staffing net margin is about 6%, against 28% for software and 18% for consulting. When roughly 94% of revenue goes to paying recruiters there is nothing left to invest in product.

**The LinkedIn tax.** Recruiter Corporate is $11,785 per seat per year. Job slots $1,025 a year. Talent Insights $6K to $20K a year. InMails past cap $10 to $15 each. Effective cost per hire from LinkedIn fees alone: $500 to $800. "Most recruiters quietly resent it. There is no negotiating leverage when there is no alternative." Sourced to Juicebox's own pricing blog.

**Recruiter bandwidth.** The median recruiter holds 22 reqs and closes 17 hires, so five reqs die in queue every year. Recruiter turnover runs 35%+ annually, meaning only about 27% of a team is the same after three years.

**Contingency is a lottery.** Average fill rate on contingent searches is around 10%. "Contingency is not a payment model. It's a prioritisation model."

**Other quotables.** 75% of resumes in any company's ATS are never looked at again. 37.9% of new hires are gone within twelve months against a standard 90-day agency guarantee, with most departures in months four to nine. A mishire costs three to five times salary. Off-limits clauses block top retained firms from 70 to 80% of the candidate companies they identified. 90% of candidates who accept a counter-offer leave anyway within a year. One in three US new hires is a returning former employee, two in three in tech.

# Part 6\. Competitive analysis

Independent research, September 2026\.

## Juicebox

Positions itself as "the leading AI recruiting platform," focused on candidate sourcing for enterprise and mid-market recruiting teams. Three core capabilities: natural-language AI search across 800M+ candidate profiles from 30+ data sources, continuous AI sourcing agents, and recruiting CRM with ATS integrations. Serves recruiting teams at 5,000+ companies, startups to Fortune 100\.

PeopleGPT is now the product name for the search component. The newer pitch is autonomy: AI Agents that "autonomously search, score candidates against role criteria, draft outreach sequences, and execute them," with recruiter approval checkpoints. Marketing claims "up to 5x recruiter efficiency and a 50% reduction in sourcing time."

Pricing: tiered from $99/month (Starter, billed annually), with a **$199/month add-on for AI Agents**. Reviewers note the platform is sourcing-only rather than full-funnel, has no native ATS, and that "additional agent fees may strain early-stage startup budgets."

## LinkedIn Recruiter

Three tiers. **Recruiter Lite** at $170/month or $1,680/year per seat, limited to 30 InMail credits monthly, third-degree connections only, no team collaboration or AI. **Professional Services (RPS)** at $6,000 to $10,000/year per seat, aimed at agencies and staffing firms, 100 InMail credits monthly, full network access. **Corporate** at $8,999 to $15,000/year per seat, 150 pooled InMails, unlimited profile views, 28+ ATS integrations.

Pain points for small operators: seat sharing is prohibited even when usage is light; at 30 monthly InMails "at 10 candidate contacts per day, you burn through the monthly allotment in three days," with roughly $10 per additional credit; total cost of ownership runs 20 to 40% above subscription once add-ons and overages compound; and roughly 15% annual price increases compound across contract periods.

## Where the gap is

Both incumbents are shaped for someone else's budget. Juicebox sells scale and autonomy to in-house teams. LinkedIn sells the network and charges per seat whether you use it or not. Neither is built around a recruiter who eats what they kill.

The incumbents compete on how much they search and how little you have to do. Noyce competes on whether the shortlist is right and whether you can prove it. Those are different axes, which is why this is winnable without outspending anyone.

Two honesty checks. Noyce is also sourcing-led, so "full-funnel" is not the argument. And per the "Why Juicebox Sells" clip, unrestricted contact data and outreach are what actually retain Juicebox customers, so those are parity requirements rather than differentiators.

# Part 7\. Positioning and messaging

## Positioning statement

> **Noyce is the candidate search built for recruiters who get paid on placements. It reads the whole brief, surfaces the people other tools can't see, and shows you exactly why each one fits.**

It names the buyer first, then the outcome, then the mechanism, because our buyer cares about the outcome and is sceptical of the mechanism.

## Completing "We help you make more money by…"

The open item from the kickoff. Three directions, strongest first. Each has to survive being said out loud to an agency owner.

1. **(Precision)** …finding the candidates your competitors' tools can't see, and proving why they fit before you spend a single outreach.  
2. **(Speed to fee)** …turning the brief you just got into a shortlist you'd put in front of the client today.  
3. **(Cost shape)** …charging you per contact you actually want, instead of thousands a year per seat. *(Blocked, see Part 8.)*

## ICP

Primary buyers are agency owners and partners who sign the tool bill and feel it personally; pod leaders who carry a desk and a small team's numbers; and independent recruiters with no procurement and no seat budget who can decide fast.

North America first. LinkedIn shows roughly 30,000 to 50,000 relevant agencies; the industry deck sizes it more precisely at about 26,000 US staffing firms with 71% of share in a long tail of sub-$10M shops.

What they have in common: paid on placements, so every claim reads as either money or noise. Cost-sensitive with no brand loyalty, so they will switch to us and switch away again. They treat tools as additive rather than replacements. They judge a tool in one search on a live role, not over a trial period.

**Vertical cut:** non-tech. Manufacturing, healthcare, legal, construction, education, skilled trades. Thejas: "areas where there is currently what I see as very underserved areas."

**Not for:** corporate or in-house talent teams, deprioritised for this launch, or procurement-driven buyers.

Saying who we are not for is doing work, not throwing away demand. It is the fastest way to sound built-for-you to a recruiter who has only ever been sold enterprise software.

## Three pillars

**1\. Precision, not reach.** Short form: *"More matches. Not more noise."*

We read the whole brief, including seniority, trajectory and pedigree, the things a recruiter reads between the lines. Keyword and filter matching doesn't get there.

Proof: the Seattle personal injury search returned 580 candidates with three named 100% matches Juicebox failed to surface at all. The legal demo video: LinkedIn 634 results with no PI experience in the top result, Noyce 564 on-spec. Healthcare: LinkedIn \~100 largely irrelevant, Noyce 300+ all matching.

**2\. A shortlist you can defend.** Short form: *"Every match comes with a reason."*

Every candidate carries a visible fit score and the reasoning behind it, so the recruiter can stand behind the shortlist in front of a client paying a fee for it.

Proof: on the Darren call the reasoning was checked live against a law firm's website and held up. Deck metric: 8 out of 10 candidates worth reaching out to. Counter-positions against the category's move toward autonomous agents that act first and explain later.

**3\. Priced like a recruiter, not a department.** Short form: *"Pay for the contact, not the seat."*

**This pillar is currently false. Do not use until pricing is settled. See Part 8\.**

**Candidate replacement, if pricing stays per-seat: Built for the 97.5%.** Tech is about 2.5% of US hires but takes roughly 80% of the recruiting industry's tooling attention. Every competitor is fighting over that sliver. Noyce is built for the rest.

## Voice and tone

Noyce should sound like a good recruiter talking shop: direct, concrete and unimpressed by hype. The reader has been sold AI recruiting software before and it didn't work, so assume mild scepticism in every line.

**Do.** Name the outcome in money or time: placements, fees, hours off a search, not abstractions. Use their vocabulary: brief, shortlist, submission, desk, split, boolean, InMail, not "talent intelligence" or "pipeline velocity." Make claims testable, because "run your hardest open role" beats "experience the difference." Show real role specs, since the law brief works precisely because it's specific enough to be hard. Write in second person and keep sentences short.

**Don't.** Lead with the technology; nobody buys the model, they buy the shortlist. Sell the roadmap; candidate playbooks, hireability scoring and interview prep are real, but they dilute a V1 message. Use a number we can't attribute; everything on the site needs a query, a date and sign-off behind it.

**Words we don't use in external copy:** productivity, recruiter enablement, efficiency, leverage, revolutionise, talent intelligence, 10x, seamless.

Two of those are decisions rather than preferences. "Productivity" was ruled out at the kickoff because it describes the feature and not the fee. "Recruiter enablement" reads as training and education, the way sales enablement does, which puts us in the wrong category. Note it is a named product layer in the investor deck, so the ban applies to market-facing copy only. A replacement term is still needed.

## Site page map

**Home** has to convince in one scroll. Hero promise, then the head-to-head proof immediately below, with the same brief run through both tools side by side. Then the three pillars in order. CTA is a real action: run your hardest open role.

**How it works** exists to kill the scepticism. Three steps: paste the brief, get a scored shortlist, launch the outreach sequence. Use a genuinely hard brief rather than a toy one, and show the fit score reasoning instead of describing it.

**Pricing** is where our ICP converts, and it is currently blocked. Once the model is settled, lead with the shape and then show the per-seat maths the buyer is living with. Don't bury it behind a "contact us."

**Who it's for** names independent recruiters and agency owners in their own words, and names the verticals. Being explicitly not for enterprise reads as focus, not limitation.

**Roadmap** is optional and low priority. If it ships: candidate playbooks, hireability scoring, outreach steps, interview prep, framed as what's coming. Must not compete with the V1 promise.

# Part 8\. Conflicts to resolve

Five things in the positioning are wrong or incomplete once the August call and the decks are taken into account. Two are business decisions for Thejas, not copy choices.

**1\. Pricing is per seat, not per contact, and pillar 3 is false.** On the Darren call Thejas quoted teams of three to five seats at a flat $999 a month, and beyond five seats $1,667 a month. Individual seats are deliberately on hold: "for an individual seat we are only focusing on teams and enterprises right now." The pitch deck separately lists "outcome-aligned per-search" pricing for the sourcing layer, and Thejas verbally offered Darren $400 a month for 500 credits plus overage. The model is genuinely in flux. As written, "Pay for the contact, not the seat" cannot go on a website.

**2\. The ICP decision and the pricing contradict each other.** The September meetings set the ICP as independent recruiters and agency pod leaders with corporates excluded. The August call has Thejas holding individual seats back to focus on teams and enterprises. The September decision is newer and presumably wins, but the price list has not moved to match it. **This is the single biggest open question in the engagement: you cannot sell a $999 a month minimum to an independent recruiter.**

**3\. The "no ATS, sourcing only" honesty check is wrong on the facts.** Thejas pitches the opposite: "it is all in one thing. So it is your top of the funnel, it's your outreach platform and it's your ATS." The deck does treat sourcing as the wedge with enablement and digital workers as later layers, so the instinct was directionally right, but the claim should be framed around the wedge rather than around a limitation.

**4\. The vertical focus was missing and is probably the strongest axis available.** Thejas aims Noyce at non-tech: manufacturing, healthcare, legal. The industry deck supplies the proof: tech is about 2.5% of all US hires but captures roughly 80% of the industry's attention and AI-tool investment. Every competitor is fighting over the 2.5%. This arguably belongs above the current pillar 1\.

**5\. The proof inventory is far stronger than previously recorded.** Both items previously marked "blocking" are largely answered. See Part 9\.

Two smaller notes. The banned-words list stands for market-facing copy, but "recruiter enablement" is a named product layer in the investor deck, so the ban needs scoping to external copy only. And the LinkedIn cost figures from the industry deck are much stronger ammunition than the ones originally used: $11,785 per seat per year, $1,025 per job slot, $10 to $15 per InMail past cap, $500 to $800 per hire in fees alone.
